What MDBs look at before they look at your technology

Korean companies usually open with the technology. Multilateral development banks open with six other questions, and they ask them in a fixed order. Answering in that order saves a year.

A good technology does not make a project. A project is a legal entity with a site, a counterparty, a set of contracts and a cash flow that can repay debt. Multilateral development banks (MDBs) and the global financial institutions that lend alongside them have seen thousands of such projects, and their credit teams have reduced what they need to see to a short list. The list is public in substance, if not in form, and it rarely changes.

The six questions

Who is the sponsor? Someone must develop, own and operate the asset for twenty years. A technology supplier is not automatically that someone. If the answer is "we will find a local partner later", the review stops here.

Where is the site, and what does it depend on? A specified plot, the right to use it, and the physical resource the project needs: treated water for cooling, irrigable land, a grid connection point. "A region" is not a site.

Does the host government want it? Not in a meeting, in writing. A letter from the ministry, the province or the operating agency, ideally pointing to the line in its own plan where the project already appears. Sovereign lending starts from the government's request, never from the vendor's proposal.

Is the technology proven at this scale? Operating references count; brochures do not. A new combination of proven parts, such as treated wastewater used for data-centre cooling, is treated as unproven until measured data exists.

Is there a feasibility study and a model? A study to international standards, and a financial model built the way a credit team reads it: debt service coverage, sensitivities, currency.

Who will do the work, and under which standards? Environmental and social standards, integrity rules such as disclosure of advisory fees, and a team with project-finance experience that holds a board mandate.

Why the order matters

Each question depends on the one before it. A feasibility study without a site studies nothing. A financial model without a sponsor models nobody's cash flow. Companies that start with the study, because it is the deliverable they know how to buy, usually have to buy it twice.

The practical consequence is that the first months of a project are spent on structure, land and government, not on engineering. That is unglamorous work, and it is where most Korean proposals we see have gaps.

What "bankable" actually means

Bankable does not mean profitable. It means a lender can underwrite the risk: the cash flow is contracted, the counterparties are creditworthy or backed, the construction risk sits with someone who can carry it, and the project survives the downside cases in the model. Many profitable ideas are not bankable, and some bankable projects are only modestly profitable. Knowing which one you have, early, is the whole point of a readiness check.

A note on timelines

A new project that goes through an MDB's sovereign window takes years from first contact to first drawdown; private-sector windows are faster but demand more from the sponsor. Plan for a process, not an application. The companies that succeed treat the first year as building the project, with the financing as its result.
