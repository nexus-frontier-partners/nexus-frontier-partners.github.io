Water infrastructure in Asia: where climate finance actually lands

Adaptation finance is the under-funded half of climate finance, and water is where most of it is spent. What makes a treatment, reuse or resilience project financeable, and what does not.

Most climate finance still goes to mitigation: power, transport, industry. Adaptation receives a smaller share despite the damage that floods, droughts and heat are already causing across Asia. Within adaptation, water is the largest line: treatment and supply, wastewater and reuse, drainage and flood protection, and the efficiency of the utilities that run them. For a company with water technology, this is the most direct route into climate finance that exists.

Why water projects are different

A water project has a utility business model. Someone pays a tariff, or a government pays on the utility's behalf, and that payment stream is what a lender underwrites. This makes water easier to structure than many adaptation projects, which have no revenue at all, and harder than power, where the offtaker is usually a single creditworthy entity.

The lender's questions are therefore about the counterparty. Is the utility creditworthy, or does the sovereign stand behind it? Is there a tariff path that covers operating costs and debt service? Is the payment in local currency, and if so, who carries the exchange risk?

Three project types that are moving

Treatment and reuse. Treating municipal or industrial wastewater to a standard that allows reuse: for irrigation, for industry, and increasingly for cooling in energy-intensive facilities such as data centres. Reuse creates a second customer for the same plant, which strengthens the revenue case.

Flood resilience and drainage. Mostly public, mostly sovereign-financed, and increasingly eligible for concessional climate finance because the adaptation benefit is clear. Technology plays a supporting role; the structure is a government project.

Utility efficiency. Reducing water losses, energy use and operating cost in existing systems. Often the fastest to finance because the asset and the customer already exist, and savings can be measured.

What makes them bankable

A specified plant and site, not a programme. A written request from the government or the utility. A tariff or payment mechanism that a credit team can model. Environmental and social work done early, because water projects touch land, communities and sometimes resettlement. And a sponsor that will stay for the life of the asset.

What does not work

Proposals that lead with the technology and leave the counterparty for later. Proposals that assume the Korean government's own development finance will fund a project the host country has not asked for. And proposals that combine several innovations at once: a new treatment process, a new reuse customer and a new financing structure. Lenders accept one new thing at a time.
