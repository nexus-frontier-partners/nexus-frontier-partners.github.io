Blended finance, explained for Korean mid-sized companies

Concessional money does not replace commercial money. It changes the risk so that commercial money will come. Here is how the pieces fit, who provides them, and what they demand in return.

Blended finance is the use of public or philanthropic capital, on concessional terms, inside a structure that is otherwise commercial. The concessional layer takes a risk that the market will not price, and the commercial layers come in on terms they can accept. The project is not subsidised into existence; it is de-risked into financeability.

The instruments

Concessional debt. A loan at below-market rates or with a longer tenor, usually from an MDB window, a climate fund or a bilateral development agency. It lowers the blended cost of capital and lengthens the repayment profile.

First-loss or subordinated capital. Equity or junior debt that absorbs losses before senior lenders. It is the instrument that most directly changes a bank's answer from no to yes.

Guarantees. Cover for specific risks, such as political risk, currency convertibility or a public counterparty's payment obligation. Guarantees do not fund the project; they make other funding possible.

Technical-assistance grants. Money for feasibility work, pilots and capacity building. Small in amount, decisive in timing: it is often the only money available before the project exists.

Who provides it

MDBs through their own concessional windows and the climate funds they administer; dedicated climate funds that invest through accredited institutions; bilateral agencies, including Korea's own concessional lending; and a growing set of philanthropic and impact investors that supply first-loss capital to fund managers in emerging markets. Each has its own eligibility rules, and most require that a project be requested or endorsed by the host country.

What it demands

Additionality. The provider must be able to show that the project would not have happened, or not on these terms, without the concessional layer. A project that is already bankable will be refused, politely.

Standards. Environmental and social performance standards, procurement rules and integrity requirements apply to the whole structure, not only to the concessional tranche. Korean companies often underestimate the documentation this creates.

Time. Concessional providers run approval cycles measured in months and governed by committees. A realistic blended structure for a new project takes one to three years from first approach to signing, and the concessional tranche is rarely the fastest part.

What it costs

Concessional does not mean free. Beyond interest and fees there is the cost of the process: advisers, studies, legal work across several jurisdictions, and management time. A structure with three providers has three sets of conditions precedent. The right question is not whether concessional money is cheaper, but whether the project can be financed at all without it.

A mid-sized company's realistic path

Start with the commercial case. If the project cannot attract a commercial lender even with a guarantee, concessional money will not save it. If it can, use the concessional layer to fix the specific risk that blocks the lender: tenor, currency, counterparty or first loss. Then build the structure around that one problem, with one lead provider, rather than collecting letters of interest from everyone. Smaller structures close; large ones with many parties mostly do not.
